# Where I come from

I grew up in the 1980s in the German Democratic Republic, a satellite state of the Soviet Union, where knowledge was a tool of the ruling class. Textbooks, songs, children's literature, school curricula—instruments of ideological indoctrination, carefully curated to shape our understanding of the world. The political class decided for us what was worth knowing.

But there was resilience. And it was rooted in stories.

Mine was a blue-collar family of farmers, bricklayers, and builders—hard workers, but also avid readers and storytellers who passed generational knowledge down through oral histories.

On my father's side were the readers. He and my grandmother kept shelves filled with books—unusual for a farming family—with narratives different from those we heard at school. They knew this was a family treasure, but one that multiplied through sharing, and so they generously lent their books to friends, neighbors, and community members. They were citizen librarians.

My mother's side of the family bore the storytellers. Her and my grandfather were gifted at sharing anecdotes, fables, and tales full of humor and street smarts, but also tragedy and loss. They kept memories alive and taught us how to reflect on the world around us, while enabling their listeners' imagination, instilling vision and hope.

These private libraries and oral histories passed across kitchen tables were my first encounters with knowledge that challenged dominant narratives. They taught me, long before I had the language for it, that access to information is never just a technical question. It is a political one, as well as one of identity.

After the Wall came down, I became the first in my family to attend university. I trained as a librarian—and came to understand that libraries are institutions built on the conviction that most knowledge should be accessible to everyone. A principle with the potential to hold democratic societies together.

That understanding, shaped by both my professional journey and the family history that inspired it, has guided everything I have done since.

## What I believe

**Knowledge is a common good.** Libraries are among the most powerful institutions we have for making that belief real — spaces where access to information is not a privilege but a right, regardless of who you are or where you come from.

My work spans open scholarship, AI literacy, and digital innovation in libraries. What connects it all is a commitment to removing barriers — whether they are paywalls, technological divides, or social exclusion — so that knowledge can serve everyone.

## What I work on

### Open Scholarship

In my work, I have managed acquisitions & collections of a university library with the purpose of breaking down barriers to information. I have co-chaired a state-wide library consortium tasked to steward the digital turn through its funding. I have overseen the operations of a university press and its strategic development towards an open access publisher. In my role as an instructor across university programs, I advocate for open practices in learning and teaching.

Open scholarship is not just a policy — it is a question of equity and inclusion.

→ [[Projects\|Acquisition Consortium for Academic Libraries]]

### Scholarly Communication

My research examines how scholars publish and how libraries can help them navigate an increasingly complex publishing landscape. I have published on how libraries help scholars resist predatory publishing and fight misinformation, and my master's thesis analyzed how the library profession engages with academic self-publishing.

→ [Fake Science … and how libraries can intervene](https://nbn-resolving.org/urn:nbn:de:0290-opus4-168032)

### Professional Ethics & Critical Practice

Libraries are built on strong values — open access, inclusion, intellectual freedom. But how well does the profession live up to them? My research asks that question. I have examined library engagement with the UN Sustainable Development Goals and whether the profession's commitment to social inclusion holds up in practice — particularly for homeless populations. My master's thesis investigated how librarians navigate the tensions between professional ethics and the realities of academic self-publishing. This critical lens drives my work: not to undermine the profession, but to strengthen it.

→ [Homeless People as Library Patrons](https://doi.org/10.18452/20199)

### AI Literacy & Education

Emerging technologies should empower, not exclude. I teach AI literacy, prompt engineering, and citizen science skills at two German universities, and I co-chair a Community of Practice on AI application for librarians at UVic. My goal is to make sure that understanding AI is not reserved for technologists but available to researchers, students, and the public.

→ [UVic Libraries, AI & Student Success](http://hdl.handle.net/1828/15694)

### Libraries as Public Infrastructure

Libraries are more than collections — they are the infrastructure of an informed society. At Kula: Library Futures Academy, I work on building the future of libraries as inclusive public institutions.

→ [Database Review: EconBiz](https://doi.org/10.1080/08963568.2023.2233837)

### Digital Preservation

Knowledge only remains a common good if it endures. I teach digitalization and long-term preservation at Leipzig University of Applied Sciences (HTWK), ensuring that today's scholarship remains accessible for future generations.
