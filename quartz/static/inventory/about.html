How It Works

  1. Explore a map of Greater Victoria centred on the downtown core
  2. Click on a building or location to open the observation form
  3. Record modernist architectural features you observe: flat roofs, ribbon windows, pilotis, extensive glazing, brise soleil, exposed concrete, and more
  4. Submit your observation to the growing community inventory

Each building record connects to three external datasets following linked open data principles:

  • Wikidata — structured knowledge about the building (architect, date, heritage status)
  • OpenStreetMap — community-mapped building footprint and location
  • BC Register of Historic Places — the provincial heritage register with 1,300+ properties in Greater Victoria

Why Mid-Century Modernism?

Victoria's mid-century modern buildings — roughly 1945 to 1975 (Segger 2019; Algie & Ashby 2007) — are increasingly at risk. While landmark structures like the BC Electric Company Building (1515 Blanshard Street, 1955, Sharp & Thompson, Berwick, Pratt) are recognized on the Canadian Register of Historic Places, hundreds of smaller modernist commercial and residential buildings remain unrecorded (Segger 2012–2017). Many lack heritage protection — in Victoria, listing on the Heritage Register alone does not restrict future actions by an owner — and face demolition or unsympathetic renovation.

Citizen science offers a way to build a comprehensive inventory faster than any single institution could manage alone (Orr et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2022). Locally, the Hallmark Heritage Society has compiled archives on over 3,500 buildings through volunteer effort since 1973.

Technology

  • Frontend: Plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with Leaflet.js for mapping. No build step.
  • Backend: Node.js and Express, deployed on Oracle Cloud (Montreal region).
  • Database: PostgreSQL + PostGIS running on the same Canadian server. No third-party database services — all data stays in Canada.

This is a proof-of-concept developed to support a grant proposal. Contributions submitted through the app are stored persistently in the database.

Feature list sources

Le Corbusier, "Five Points of a New Architecture" (1927). The theoretical origin of pilotis, ribbon windows, free facade (extensive glazing), and flat roof. See Wikipedia summary.
US National Park Service, Preservation Brief 17: "Architectural Character — Identifying the Visual Aspects of Historic Buildings." The standard methodology for visual building surveys: shape, roof, projections, recesses, openings, materials. nps.gov
DOCOMOMO US, "Styles of the Modern Era." Character-defining feature lists for International Style, Brutalist, Mid-Century Modern, and other sub-styles. docomomo-us.org
DOCOMOMO US, "How to Evaluate Modern." Evaluation framework covering technological, social, artistic, canonic, and referential merit. docomomo-us.org
Parks Canada, Standards and Guidelines for the Conservation of Historic Places in Canada, 2nd ed. (2011). Canadian national standard. Addresses modern materials and assemblies. historicplaces.ca
SurveyLA / "L.A. Modernism, 1919–1980." The largest modern-era field survey (880,000 parcels) with citizen-accessible feature categories. docomomo-us.org
UNESCO / ICOMOS / DOCOMOMO Joint Programme on Modern Heritage. International framework for modern heritage identification. whc.unesco.org

Design decisions

Why "extensive glazing" instead of "curtain wall"? A curtain wall is defined by its non-structural relationship to the frame — invisible from the street. "Extensive glazing" captures what citizens actually see.
Why separate "flat roof" and "unusual roof shape"? Roof form is the single most visible MCM identifier (NPS Brief 17). Butterfly and folded-plate roofs are equally distinctive and shouldn't be collapsed with flat roofs.
Why include "decorative screen"? Breeze blocks are among the most recognisable MCM features but absent from Le Corbusier's five points (they became widespread in the 1950s–60s).
